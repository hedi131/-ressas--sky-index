How the score works

The six factors

FactorWeightWhat it measures
Cloud canvas30 ptsMid & high-altitude cloud cover above the summit. This is what catches and reflects color — zero cloud gives a flat, "lifeless" gradient; full overcast kills it from the other direction.
Horizon window18 ptsLow-altitude cloud cover directly above the summit. Needs to stay clear so direct light can reach the underside of the higher clouds at a low angle.
Downrange corridor12 ptsLow cloud cover at two points 60km and 130km out, along the sun's actual computed bearing for that event. Checks whether something is blocking the light well before it even reaches you — this is the closest honest proxy to "cloud direction" that real forecast data supports.
Atmospheric clarity20 ptsRelative humidity + visibility. Haze mutes saturation and contrast even on an otherwise good cloud day.
Dust / aerosols10 ptsPM10 & dust concentration. Light Saharan dust can deepen reds; heavy dust events wash the whole sky out.
Wind10 ptsLight-to-moderate wind keeps the air clean without shredding cloud structure; dead calm risks stagnant haze, strong wind risks a wiped-clean sky.

Why a distant "Exceptional" can settle lower as the day approaches

The canvas score checks three points — local, 60km, and 130km — and can credit whichever one shows the best cloud structure, since a wide mountaintop view can be dominated by cloud that isn't directly overhead. But taking the best of three separately-uncertain forecasts is a known statistical bias: it tends to overshoot the true value, and the overshoot is worse the more uncertain each forecast is. Forecast uncertainty is highest at long lead times. So a score that's high specifically because a distant checkpoint beat the local reading is discounted the further out it is — full weight inside a day or two, progressively less trust further out — and settles toward the local reading's own number as confidence in the corridor forecast is lower. This means scores should generally look more conservative several days out and firm up (up or down) as the event gets closer, rather than starting inflated and only ever drifting down.

How the direction is calculated

Each event's true sunrise/sunset compass bearing is computed from the summit's latitude and the sun's declination for that date, using a standard solar-geometry formula (verified against three known reference cases — equinox gives due East/West everywhere, and mid-latitude solstice bearings were checked against published almanac values before this went live). Two checkpoints are placed 60km and 130km along that bearing using standard great-circle geodesy, and their forecast low cloud cover is pulled at the exact moment of the event. The bearing is held fixed across the whole 10-day window rather than recalculated per day — it drifts at most a few degrees over that span, which shifts a checkpoint by only a few km at this distance.

Cloud inversion ("sea of clouds") signal

This is deliberately kept separate from the 0-100 score, because it's a different kind of good outcome, not more color. It answers a different question: is there a shallow cloud layer trapped below the summit, with the peak sitting clear above it? The check compares the summit's own cloud reading against a second reading for the same coordinates with elevation pulled down to a generic valley-floor height. If the valley reading shows real cloud while the summit stays clear, the badge appears. Be clear-eyed about what this is: Open-Meteo's elevation parameter adjusts the forecast using standard lapse-rate relationships, it does not simulate an actual vertical atmospheric profile. This is a reasonable proxy for a real, physically meaningful pattern — it is not a direct measurement of one. Inversions are genuinely difficult to forecast precisely even for professional meteorologists; treat the badge as a reason to check back closer to the day, not as a guarantee.

Alpenglow / Belt of Venus signal

Every other factor on this page looks toward the sun. This one looks the opposite way. Around sunrise and sunset there's often a pink-purple band low on the horizon opposite the sun, with a soft blue-grey shadow beneath it — and it doesn't need dramatic clouds to show up, just reasonably clear air in that direction. The check places one reference point 50km out along the bearing directly opposite the sun and reads its low cloud cover as a proxy for whether that part of the sky is open. It's shown as a separate badge because it can be genuinely worth turning around for even on a day the main score rates as flat and unremarkable.

Reading the score

  • 85–100 — Exceptional: textured, saturated sky likely. Go.
  • 70–84 — Good: solid odds of real color and structure.
  • 50–69 — Average: watchable, probably unremarkable.
  • 30–49 — Below average: likely flat or hazy.
  • 0–29 — Poor: skip it.
Honest limitations: this is a forecast-driven heuristic, not a certainty. Confidence is high for the next 1–2 days, medium out to day 4, and meaningfully looser beyond that — the underlying weather model itself gets less reliable with lead time, not the scoring logic. The downrange corridor still can't see cloud cover as a spatial map, only total-sky-fraction at three separate points along one line — it's a real improvement over ignoring direction entirely, but it isn't a satellite image. Checkpoints that land over open water may snap to the nearest coastal grid cell (a detail of the underlying weather service), slightly reducing precision for that specific case — this is more likely for sunrise checkpoints near the coast than for sunset checkpoints further inland.
